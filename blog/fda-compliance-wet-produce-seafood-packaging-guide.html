FDA Compliance for Wet Produce and Seafood Packaging

Waxproduceboxes Editorial • October 2026

Packaging that touches fresh produce or seafood is a food-contact article, and that puts it squarely under food-safety scrutiny. For procurement and quality teams, choosing a box is not just a logistics decision—it is a compliance decision. Wax-coated corrugated and reusable plastic boxes are held to very different standards in practice, and understanding those differences protects you during audits, recalls, and customer qualification. This guide covers what FDA compliance actually requires for wet-produce and seafood packaging and how material choice shapes your risk.

What Food-Contact Compliance Means

In the United States, materials that contact food must be made from substances that are either generally recognized as safe or cleared for food contact under FDA regulations. For plastic boxes, the practical requirement is that the resin and any colorants or additives comply with the applicable food-contact provisions of 21 CFR. Polypropylene is widely used for food contact precisely because compliant grades are well established. The key for buyers is documentation: you want a supplier who can state, in writing, that the resin used is FDA-compliant for food contact. Why Material Choice Drives Sanitation

Wet produce and seafood generate the hardest hygiene challenge in the cold chain: standing water, organic residue, and repeated temperature swings. Waxed corrugated cannot be effectively washed and sanitized—it absorbs moisture, degrades, and is discarded after one use, which sidesteps the cleaning question but creates a fresh box of unknown handling history each trip. Reusable PP boxes take the opposite approach: they are designed to be washed, sanitized, and verified between uses. Smooth, non-absorbent surfaces and draining geometry mean wash water sheds cleanly and bacteria have nowhere to harbor. For integrated cold chain and moisture-resistant packaging, that washability is central to a defensible food-safety program.

Designing for Hygienic Performance

Compliance is not only about the resin—it is also about box design. Features that support sanitation and audits include:

  • Rounded internal corners that eliminate crevices where residue collects.
  • Full drainage so no pooled water remains after washing.
  • Smooth, non-porous walls that do not absorb juices or fishy odors.
  • Durable surfaces that tolerate caustic and sanitizer chemistries without crazing.

These features let you build a written cleaning and verification protocol—something auditors increasingly expect for reusable assets.

Traceability and Recall Readiness

Modern food-safety expectations include traceability. Reusable boxes can carry molded-in identifiers or applied RFID and barcode tags, giving you the ability to track which assets moved which product—valuable during a recall or a sanitation deviation. A single-use wax box offers no such continuity. When you source from a direct manufacturer, you can also request documentation tying each production lot to a specific plant and resin batch, strengthening your traceability chain from the packaging side.

Temperature and Chemical Resistance

Seafood and wet produce packaging must survive icing, hydrocooling, and aggressive wash chemistry. Compliant PP maintains its properties across the refrigerated and near-freezing range and resists the sanitizers used in food plants, so the box that passes your initial qualification keeps performing trip after trip. Waxed fiberboard, by contrast, softens and fails under the same conditions, which can compromise both the produce and your compliance posture if a box collapses mid-cold-chain.

Documentation Auditors Actually Ask For

When a third-party auditor or a retail customer evaluates your program, they rarely take a verbal assurance that packaging is food-safe. They ask for paper. Build a packaging compliance file that includes the supplier's statement of FDA-compliant resin, the specific food-contact regulations referenced, any migration or food-contact test summaries available, your written cleaning and sanitation protocol with verification records, and documentation of the manufacturing origin. Having this file assembled before an audit turns a potential finding into a non-event. A direct manufacturer relationship makes assembling it far easier, because you are one step from the plant rather than several steps removed through a broker who may not be able to produce the underlying certifications on demand.
